Construct the successive approximations to the solution $\phi$ of the differential equation $y' = -y$ that satisfies $\phi(0)=2$ and $\phi_0(t) = t$. Do these successive approximations converge to a familiar function, and if so, is this function a solution of the problem?
$$y'= -y, \phi(0) = 2$$

We have $y'=f(t,y)=-y$

The initial condition is $\phi(0)=2$. Hence $t_0 = 0, y_0 =2$

We have a constant function $\phi_0(t) = y_0 = 2$.

The successive approximation is

$$\phi_{j + 1}(t) = y 0 + \int_{t_0}^t f(s, \phi_j (s))ds, j=0, 1, 2, \cdots$$ $$\phi_0(t) = 2$$ \begin{align} \phi_{1}(t) &= y_{0} + \int_0^t f(s, \phi_{0}(s)) ds\\ &= 2 + \int_0^t -\phi_{0}(s) ds\\ &= 2 - \int_0^t 2 ds\\ &= 2 - \sbracket{2s}_0^t\\ &= 2 - 2t \end{align} \begin{align} \phi_{2}(t) &= y_{0} + \int_0^t f(s, \phi_{1}(s)) ds\\ &= 2 + \int_0^t -\phi_{1}(s) ds\\ &= 2 - \int_0^t (2 - 2s) ds\\ &= 2 - \sbracket{2s - s^2}_0^t\\ &= 2 - 2t + t^2 \\ &= 2 - 2t + 2\frac{t^2}{2!} \\ \end{align} \begin{align} \phi_{3}(t) &= y_{0} + \int_0^t f(s, \phi_{2}(s)) ds\\ &= 2 + \int_0^t -\phi_{2}(s) ds\\ &= 2 - \int_0^t (2 - 2s + 2\frac{s^2}{2!}) ds\\ &= 2 - \sbracket{2s - 2\frac{s^2}{2} + 2 \frac{s^3}{3!} }_0^t\\ &= 2 - 2t + 2 \frac{t^2}{2!} - 2 \frac{t^3}{3!} \end{align} \begin{align} \phi_{4}(t) &= y_{0} + \int_0^t f(s, \phi_{3}(s)) ds\\ &= 2 + \int_0^t -\phi_{3}(s) ds\\ &= 2 - \int_0^t (2 - 2s + 2 \frac{s^2}{2!} - 2 \frac{s^3}{3!}) ds\\ &= 2 - \sbracket{2s - 2\frac{s^2}{2!} + 2\frac{s^3}{3!} - 2 \frac{s^4}{4!} }_0^t\\ &= 2 - 2t + 2\frac{t^2}{2!} - 2 \frac{t^3}{3!} + 2 \frac{t^4}{4!} \end{align}

Assume that $$\phi_n(t) = 2 - 2t + 2\frac{t^2}{2!} - 2 \frac{t^3}{3!} + 2 \frac{t^4}{4!} + \cdots + 2 \frac{t^n}{n!}$$

\begin{align} \phi_{n+1}(t) &= y_{0} + \int_0^t f(s, \phi_{n}(s)) ds\\ &= 2 + \int_0^t -\phi_{n}(s) ds\\ &= 2 - \int_0^t (2 - 2s + 2\frac{s^2}{2!} - 2 \frac{s^3}{3!} + \cdots + 2 \frac{s^n}{n!}) ds\\ &= 2 + \sbracket{ 2s - 2 \frac{s^2}{2!} + 2\frac{s^3}{3!} - 2 \frac{s^4}{4!} + \cdots + 2 \frac{s^{n+1}}{(n+1)!} }_0^t\\ &= 2 + 2t - 2 \frac{t^2}{2!} + 2\frac{t^3}{3!} - 2 \frac{t^4}{4!} + \cdots + 2 \frac{t^{n+1}}{(n+1)!}\\ \end{align}

Thus $\phi_n(s)$ is valid for every $n=0,1,2, \cdots$.

\begin{align} \phi_n(t) &= 2 - 2t + 2\frac{t^2}{2!} - 2 \frac{t^3}{3!} + 2 \frac{t^4}{4!} + \cdots + 2 \frac{t^n}{n!} \\ &= 2 + 2(-t) + 2\frac{(-t)^2}{2!} + 2 \frac{(-t)^3}{3!} + 2 \frac{(-t)^4}{4!} + \cdots + 2 \frac{(-t)^n}{n!} \\ &= \sum_{k=0}^{n} 2 \frac{(-t)^k}{k!} \\ &= 2 \sum_{k=0}^{n} \frac{(-t)^k}{k!} \\ \end{align} \begin{align} \limit \phi_n(t) &= \limit 2 \sum_{k=0}^{n} \frac{(-t)^k}{k!} \\ &= 2 \limit \sum_{k=0}^{n} \frac{(-t)^k}{k!} \\ &= 2 \sum_{k=0}^{\infty} \frac{(-t)^k}{k!} \\ &= 2 e^{-t} \end{align}

Yes, these successive approximations converge to a familiar function.

\begin{align} \text{Let } \phi(t) &= 2 e^{-t} \\ \phi'(t) &= - 2 e^{-t} = - \phi(t) \\ \phi(0) &= 2 e^{0} = 2 \\ \end{align}

Therefore $\phi(t)$ is a solution of given differential equation with initial condition $\phi(0)=2$.

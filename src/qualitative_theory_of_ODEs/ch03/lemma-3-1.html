--- title: "Lemma 3.1" ---

{{ title }}

If $\phi$ is a solution of the initial value problem $$y' = f(t,y), \phi (t_0) = y_0$$ on an interval $I$, then $\phi$ satisfies $$y(t) = y_{0} + \int_{t_0}^{t} f(s, y(s)) ds \tag{a}$$ on $I$. Conversely, if $y(t)$ is a solution of $y(t) = y_{0} + \int_{t_0}^{t} f(s, y(s)) ds$ on some interval $J$ containing $t_{0}$ then $y(t)$ satisfies $y' = f(t, y)$ on $J$ and also the initial condition $\phi(t_0) = y_0$.

Suppose $\phi$ is a solution of the problem $y' = f(t,y)$ on $I$ satisfying the initial condition $\phi (t_0) = y_0$.

$\phi' (t) = f(t, \phi(t))$ for any $t \in I$.

Integrating from $t_{0}$ to any $t$ on $I$, we obtain \begin{align} \phi(t) - \phi(t_{0}) &= \int_{t_0}^{t} f(s, \phi(s)) ds \\ \phi(t) &= \phi(t_{0}) + \int_{t_0}^{t} f(s, \phi(s)) ds \\ \phi(t) &= y_0 + \int_{t_0}^{t} f(s, \phi(s)) ds \end{align}

Thus $\phi$ satisfies $$y(t) = y_{0} + \int_{t_0}^{t} f(s, y(s)) ds$$ on $I$.

Conversely, suppose $y(t)$ is a continuous solution of $y(t) = y_{0} + \int_{t_0}^{t} f(s, y(s)) ds$ on some interval $J$ containing $t_{0}$.

$y(t)$ is differentiable by the continuity of the function $f(s, y(s))$ under the integral.

By the fundamental theorem of calculus,

\begin{align} y(t) &= y_{0} + \int_{t_0}^{t} f(s, y(s)) ds \\ y'(t) &= 0 + f(t, y(t)) \frac{dt}{dt} - f(t_0, y(t_0)) \frac{dt_0}{dt} \\ y'(t) &= f(t, y(t)) \end{align}

Thus $y(t)$ satisfies $y' = f(t, y(t))$.

Putting $t= t_0$ in equation A gives

\begin{align} y(t) &= y_{0} + \int_{t_0}^{t} f(s, y(s)) ds \\ y(t_0) &= y_{0} + \int_{t_0}^{t_0} f(s, y(s)) ds \\ y(t_0) &= y_0 \end{align}

Therefore $y(t)$ satisfies $y' = f(t, y)$ on $J$ and also the initial condition $\phi(t_0) = y_0$.

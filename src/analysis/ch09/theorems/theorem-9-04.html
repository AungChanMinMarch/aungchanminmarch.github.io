--- title: "9.4 Definitions: Linear Transformation" ---

{{title}}

A mapping $A$ of a vector space $X$ into a vector space $Y$ is said to be a linear transformation if $$A(\bvec{x}_1 + \bvec{x}_2) = A\bvec{x}_1 + A\bvec{x}_2, A(c\bvec{x}) = cAx\bvec{x}$$ for all $\bvec{x}, \bvec{x}_1, \bvec{x}_2 \in X$ and all scalars $c$.

OR

$$A(c_1 \bvec{x}_1 + c_2 \bvec{x}_2) = c_1 A\bvec{x}_1 + c_2 A\bvec{x}_2 \text{ for all } \bvec{x}_1, \bvec{x}_2 \in X \text{ and all scalars } c$$

Note that one often writes $A\bvec{x}$ instead of $A(\bvec{x})$ if $A$ is linear.

Prove that $A\bvec{0} = \bvec{0}$ if $A$ is linear.

Take any $\bvec{x} \in X$. We have

\begin{align} A \bvec{0} &= A (\bvec{x} - \bvec{x}) \\ &= A (\bvec{x} + (-\bvec{x})) \\ &= A \bvec{x} + A(-\bvec{x}) \\ &= A \bvec{x} - A(\bvec{x}) \\ &= \bvec{0} \end{align}

Observe also that a linear transformation $A$ of $X$ into $Y$ is completely determined by its action on any basis: If $\{ \bvec{x}_1 , \dots , \bvec{x}_n \}$ is a basis of $X$, then every $\bvec{x} \in X$ has a unique representation of the form

$$\bvec{x}= \sum_{i=1}^n c_i \bvec{x}_i$$

and if $A:X \to Y$ is a linear transformation,

\begin{align} \bvec{x} &= \sum_{i=1}^n c_i \bvec{x}_i \\ &= c_1 \bvec{x}_1 + \dots + c_n \bvec{x}_n \\ A \bvec{x} &= A (\bvec{x}) \\ &= A(c_1 \bvec{x}_1 + \dots + c_n \bvec{x}_n) \\ &= A(c_1 \bvec{x}_1) + \dots + A(c_n \bvec{x}_n) \\ &= c_1 A(\bvec{x}_1) + \dots + c_n A(\bvec{x}_n) \\ &= c_1 A\bvec{x}_1 + \dots + c_n A\bvec{x}_n \\ &= \sum_{i=1}^n c_i A \bvec{x}_i \end{align}

Linear transformations of $X$ into $X$ are often called linear operators on $X$.

If $A$ is a linear operator on $X$ which is one-to-one and onto, we say that A is invertible. In this case we can define an operator $A^{-1}$ on $X$ by requiring that $A^{-1}(A \bvec{x}) = \bvec{x}$ for all $x \in X$.

Let $A$ be a linear operator on $X$. If $A^{-1}$ exists, then show that $A^{-1}$ is linear.

Since $A^{-1}$ exists, $A^{-1}(A \bvec{x}) = \bvec{x}$ and $A^{-1}(A \bvec{y}) = \bvec{y}$ for all $\bvec{x}, \bvec{y} \in X$. Then, for all $\bvec{x}, \bvec{y} \in X$ and all scalars $c$,

\begin{align} A^{-1}(A\bvec{x} + A \bvec{y}) &= A^{-1}(A(\bvec{x} + \bvec{y})) \\ &= \bvec{x} + \bvec{y} \\ &= A^{-1}(A\bvec{x}) + A^{-1}(A \bvec{y}) \\ \end{align} \begin{align} \text{ and } A^{-1}(cA\bvec{x}) &= A^{-1}(A(c\bvec{x})) \\ &= c\bvec{x} \\ &= c A^{-1}(A \bvec{x}) \\ \end{align}

Hence $A^{-1}$ is linear.
